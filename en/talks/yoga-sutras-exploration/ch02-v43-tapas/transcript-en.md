## Opening

> *CHANT — the verse, sung once: kāyendriya-siddhir aśuddhi-kṣayāt tapasaḥ*

Thank you, Janardhana. Gracias. *Arigatō gozaimasu.* *Hari Om Tat Sat.* If you can hear my voice, I can see some people responding already. Thank you.

We continue our series of reflections on the *Yoga Sūtras*, with theory and practice, and today is the fruit of the *niyama* *tapas*. In every class we look to read one or two translations, to say something about the topic and its focus, and to experiment with how we can bring that *sūtra* into an actual practice, into daily life somehow. So this is my offering, but really the invitation is for you to use it as an idea, a starting point for how to adapt it to your own practice and your own life.

Before saying much more, let's shift into an attitude for *sādhana* by preparing for *Śāntipāṭha*.

## Preparing for Śāntipāṭha

> *PRACTICE — Settling: posture, body, breath, the inner flame, then OM ×3 and the opening mantras*

As discipline is one of the themes of this *sūtra*, start to move through the discipline of preparing your body, breath and mind, in such a way that it can contact a space of stillness and balance, as well as subtle awareness and internalization.

The ideal physical arrangement is one that supports an upright, aligned spine, where the skeletal system allows the torso, arms, neck and head to be completely relaxed. The hands in a *mudrā*, a gesture of your choice, resting on the knees, thighs or lap; the mouth gently closed, and, if the environment allows, the eyes lightly closed as well.

Take some time just to feel into your whole body, to be aware of the range of experiences or sensations that may be arising within, including areas of comfort, discomfort and neutrality.

Be aware of the gross points of contact between the base of the body and the floor; the subtler points of contact between the hands and the fingers in the *mudrā*; and the even subtler points of contact between the closed eyelids.

Now direct that subtle awareness to the natural breath. Once you have found it, actually begin to feel it, not only at the entrance of the nasal passage, but as far into yourself as you can follow it, and as far out of the body as you can imagine it coming out.

Visualize yourself as you are sitting now, and gaze inwards to the eyebrow centre, turning the eyes in and up to look at that point. Imagine that there, in front of the eyebrow centre, is a table with a lamp and a flame. Gazing at that flame, its shape, its colours, we will chant the mantra *OM* three times together, and the opening *śānti* mantras together as well.

With each repetition of the mantra *OM*, imagine that the light coming from that inner flame grows larger and brighter. A slow, full breath in to chant the mantra *OM*.

> *CHANT — OM ×3*

> *CHANT — the opening mantras, together, closing with Om Śānti Śānti Śānti, Hari Om*

Bring the hands into the prayer position, the thumbs in contact with the heart. Take a very deep breath in and out.

> *PRACTICE — palming (first round)*

Move into a good round of palming, with the intention of bringing heat to the palms. And once you have warmth to share, bring those palms close to the closed eyes. Feel the sensations of warmth, absorb them, and breathe that into yourself. Exhaling, lower the hands, and then slowly open the eyes. *Hari Om Tat Sat.*

## The Sutra

I understand it's still chilly there, so I will try to move right through the theory and get to the practice. Please, during this theory session, feel free to move the body as you need, and move in and out of palming any time you feel chilly.

We're focusing on chapter two of the *Yoga Sūtras*, verse 43 today. And you all heard the excellent chanting of this very verse at the beginning of the session.

> *kāyendriya-siddhir aśuddhi-kṣayāt tapasaḥ*
> — *Yoga Sūtra* II.43

### The words of the sutra

*Kāya* — like *kāya sthairyam* — is body. It is a word that comes up in our practices, an easy one to memorize: *kāya sthairyam*, body stillness. *Kāya* is body.

*Indriya* is also an excellent word to know. It means the senses. We have gross senses and subtle senses. We have the *karma-indriyas*, the senses that do work, and the *jñāna-indriyas*, the senses that perceive and connect us to the world.

*Siddhi* is often translated as "psychic powers", but in reality it means to achieve perfection: attainment, achievement, mastery over something. And this is important to remember, because it takes the *sūtra* out of the legendary and brings it to something we can relate to. Each one of us has many *siddhis*: having learned to walk, having learned to think, and maybe even having learned how to feel.

*Śuddhi* is a word we came across in an earlier *sūtra*, meaning purity. *Aśuddhi* negates that, to mean impurity. And you hear this in our closing *Śāntipāṭha*: *sat* means truth, and *asat* means untruth, or falseness.

I'm going to skip the next word, because I'm not so familiar with it, and move to *tapas*.

And so this gives us the translation: from *tapas*, as impurity dwindles or reduces, body and senses become perfected.

### Swami Satyananda's translation

I want to read the full translation from Swami Satyananda Saraswati at least once, so we all hear it.

> By practising austerities, impurities are destroyed, and there comes perfection in the body and sense organs.
> — *[Swami Satyananda Saraswati,* Four Chapters on Freedom*, pp. 202–203]*

His focus in the commentary he gives is that we want this for better meditation. I quote him:

> If you want to meditate for a long time every day, you must have a perfect body.
> — *[Swami Satyananda Saraswati,* Four Chapters on Freedom*, pp. 202–203]*

And as he has shared in many of his *satsangs*, it is from that meditative state that real, deep insights and transformations can take place. So that is the motivation.

### What the austerities are

Some of the austerities he mentions in the commentary: exposing the body to the environment; doing various *prāṇāyāmas* to create internal heat; developing the fire of concentration, or one-pointedness; and even the fire of disciplines such as fasting. These pursuits, he says, remove toxins and strengthen the body and the willpower, to become fit for meditation.

And this is third in our sequence of the *niyamas*, after *śauca* and *santoṣa*.

### Tapas, from Vyāsa to today

Classically, Vyāsa, who was a very famous *ṛṣi*, gives us the idea of *tapas* meaning austerity: the classical idea of a yogi withstanding any hardship. Modern yogis and gurus such as Swami Satyananda have framed it in the context of austerities that make us fit for higher *sādhana*.

And in our personal, modern context, those higher *sādhanas* unlock the inner potential to pursue our desires and to remain balanced amidst any activity, including family life, work life and personal life.

Although today we will be doing practices from *haṭha yoga* and touching *rāja yoga*, I invite you, in your own time, to see how *tapas* can be applied to all the branches of yoga.

### Willpower

One of the ideas that is also important to keep throughout the practice, which we are going to start shortly, is this idea of willpower. *Tapas* requires and generates willpower. Willpower is considered the first step of being able to achieve anything we want, simple or demanding.

And we need the knowledge of the steps required: *jñāna śakti*. And we need the ability to actually start and act on that process: *kriyā śakti*.

Please take this awareness into each and every practice today. Before you initiate any movement of the body, the breath or the mind, try to feel: where does it come from? Am I intentional in this action, or is it automatic, or what is the proportion of each? And can I follow the action to the end? Or what are the barriers that prevent me from doing that calmly and steadily?

## Entering the Practice

Wonderful. So now we start the practice session, and I've put some images there just as a reference.

Hopefully most of these are practices you know. Of course, if something is new or overly demanding, please listen to your body and your intuition. Limiting the range of movement, using any body or external support, or limiting the rounds are great ways to adapt. And with each of these practices, keep in mind this principle of *tapas* generating heat, light and purification.

## Palming — Heat, Light, Purification

> *PRACTICE — palming: three rounds (10, 20 and 30 seconds)*

Our first practice is palming, which can be done with the legs straight, cross-legged, or in *vajrāsana*.

Bringing the hands together, rub them vigorously for at least ten seconds, trying to create heat. Now, when you cover the eyes, immediately connect to the sensation of warmth. Notice any colours that may appear in *cidākāśa*, and see if you can steer them towards a sense of brightness. Draw that warmth and light in through the eyes into your brain with a deep inhalation, and exhale, lowering the hands.

Second round, twenty seconds. Begin another round of palming. Notice where you feel any fatigue, if at all, any soreness. How can you intensify the sense of heat?

And now cover the eyes, immediately connect with the sensation of warmth, and visualize light. Allow both the sensations and the visualization to clear *cidākāśa* of any mental activity within that space. Draw that warmth and light in through the eyes into the midbrain, exhaling, lowering the hands.

And the third and final round, thirty seconds. Begin. Try to make it even hotter. Does that come just from the speed and pressure of the hands? Or is it assisted by the breath? Or by the mind willing the heat to be there?

Palms to eyes, connect with the sensations of warmth, visualize light. Allow that warmth and light to wash over the space of *cidākāśa*. Like a star in space exploding, imagine that going in all directions of the mind. And with the final deep breath in, breathe the warmth and light into the midbrain, and, exhaling, lower the hands.

## Opening and Closing the Hands

> *PRACTICE — hand opening and closing: five light rounds, five rounds with squeeze and hold, then the Bodhidharma version*

Adjust the sitting position as needed for opening and closing the hands. First five rounds, very light, perfect movements: inhaling as you open and spread, exhaling as you gently close around the thumbs.

For the next five rounds, on the exhalation only, you're going to hold with the breath out for a mental count of three, and squeeze tighter. So inhale together and open; exhaling, close, and keep tight, tighter than usual, for a mental count of three.

Notice how deeply you feel it, up into the arms and down through the body. Grip strength is considered highly correlated with longevity. We don't know why yet.

Make this your last round, whatever number you have reached.

### A martial-arts version

And for our final version: this is a martial-arts version, said to have been adopted from the *sannyāsī* Bodhidharma when he entered China from India. It is a variation that generates a lot of willpower and strength.

Today we do five rounds, but normally we train up to forty-nine. Please do it with me. Inhale and open the fingers. Exhaling, gently close the fists. With the next inhalation, keep the tightness that you currently have. Exhaling, tighten as if you're squeezing something in your hands. The fists stay closed. Inhaling, keep that tension.

On the third breath out, squeeze even tighter. Inhaling, maintain that tension. Repeating for two more rounds, keep squeezing tighter on the exhalation, so that by the fifth round you're squeezing absolutely as hard as you can.

When finished, take a very deep breath in, and exhale as long as you can while doing palming, so you're only palming for as long as you can breathe out. As you begin inhaling, cover the eyes, and breathe in all that warmth and *prāṇa*.

## Pāda Sañcālanāsana — Austerity in the Legs

> *PRACTICE — pāda sañcālanāsana (leg cycling): one minute forward, rest, one minute in reverse*

Come into a lying position for the warm-up of *Pāda Sañcālanāsana*. Palms down; the hands can slide underneath the buttocks for extra support. A fantastic practice for generating heat in the body and awakening the abdominal system and the *maṇipūra* area.

Small circles if you have any lower back or neck conditions; otherwise large circles, for maximum austerity. Start to move as if you're on a bicycle, on a bicycle journey, large circles with both legs cycling.

We're going to be here for at least a minute, so be aware of your breath, be aware of your body, and really feel and connect with the practice.

Sometimes in *tapas*-themed classes, we have students do this until they simply cannot do it. I'm not recommending that now, but that is the mentality, the attitude, that we can adopt sometimes when working with *tapasya*: how to approach what might currently seem impossible to us.

### Rest, and the reverse

Allow the soles of the feet to rest on the floor. Knees remain bent. Let the breath be completely natural, the body still, and observe yourself in this state.

What can you adjust for the reverse cycling of one minute, so that you are just able to finish and give everything you have: not under-giving, not over-giving?

Swami Vivekananda says rest is a change of activity, not an absence of activity. So now, change to backward cycling, one minute. Begin.

Apply willpower in the discipline of the movement of the joints and legs. Apply *jñāna śakti* in your awareness of the principles of safety and the actual mechanics of this practice, and *kriyā śakti* in the integrity of maintaining effort throughout.

## Śavāsana — Reading the Koshas

> *PRACTICE — śavāsana with a reflection through the five koshas*

Relax the legs onto the floor. Come into *Śavāsana*, body still, breath natural. Observe yourself.

What do you feel in the whole *annamaya kośa*, the physical body?

What are you experiencing in the *prāṇamaya kośa*, the whole pranic, energetic body?

In what way are you aware of *manomaya kośa*, the mental body, including the senses, the *indriyas*?

Has anything been communicated by the *vijñānamaya kośa*, the insight body, the intuition body, the wisdom body?

And finally, does this space of relaxation offer you contact with the causal body, the bliss body, where you can just be?

---

## Naukāsana — The Boat

> *PRACTICE — naukāsana (boat pose): five rounds with a lengthening hold*

Our next practice is *Naukāsana*, the boat pose. For anyone with lower back or neck conditions, a bent knee with the soles flat on the floor is recommended, with the hands on the thighs. Otherwise, both legs and the torso, neck and head will be raised when we begin.

With each round, we extend the duration of the breath hold and the body hold. When you find your limit, please do not force yourself beyond it. Recognize it and work within it.

Legs together, hands on the thighs, a deep breath in. Hold, and raise.

Ten, nine, eight, seven, six, five, four, three, two, one. Relax and breathe out.

You can take a deep breath in and out between each round.

Starting round two: deep breath in, hold and raise.

Twelve, eleven, ten, nine, eight, seven, six, five, four, three, two, one, zero. Relax, breathe out. Deep breath in and out.

Round three: deep breath in, hold and raise.

Fourteen, thirteen, twelve, eleven, ten, nine, eight, seven, six, five, four, three, two, one. Relax, breathe out. Deep inhalation, exhalation.

Round four: inhale, lock and raise. The chin is down towards the chest, the spine is strong, the heels a few centimetres off the ground, the fingers pointing towards the toes, the eyes open. Relax, exhale, then a deep breath.

And the final round: deep breath in, hold and raise.

Twenty, nineteen, eighteen, seventeen, sixteen, fifteen, fourteen, thirteen, twelve, eleven, ten, nine, eight, seven, six, five, four…

> *CHANT — OM ×3*

Relax. Breathe out.

## Śavāsana — Tapas in Rest

> *PRACTICE — śavāsana: tapas in the resting state*

*Śavāsana.* Observe the body. Observe the breath. Observe the mind. Observe the intuition.

Not that it should be a goal, but how might life be different if we could sustain this type of engagement throughout the day, and even throughout the night, in our subtler states of consciousness?

Most of us understand hardship in waking life. But what is *tapas* in the resting state? How does rest provide purification? Contact with willpower.

Is it easier to remain attached, or to let go? A sincere, open question.

### Coming back up

We start to move our fingers; please move your fingers and toes, your knees and elbows, neck and head. Stretch the body into a long line, and then, exhaling, into a small ball, hugging yourself.

Rolling side to side a few times, then forward and back. Maybe you can roll into a squat and stand up; otherwise, in your own way, come into a standing position.

## Utthānāsana — Holding the Squat

> *PRACTICE — utthānāsana (squat holds): three descents, then the middle stage held for five long breaths*

Extend the legs out to at least mat width, with the toes turned outwards, the feet turned outwards. When you lower the buttocks a few centimetres, notice in which direction the knees bend. That's the angle you want for the feet.

We start in a standing position, hands interlaced in front of the body, hanging.

Inhale deeply. Exhaling, bend the knees and lower about ten centimetres, or a third of the way down to the floor. Hold with the breath out for a mental count of five. Then, inhaling, raise back up with control.

Exhaling, lower two thirds of your maximum towards the floor. Hold for a mental count of five, and then inhale and raise with control.

Exhaling again, lower to your personal maximum, keeping in mind ankles, knees and back. Hold for a mental count of five. Inhale with control, back up.

This can be done for numerous rounds. Today we're going to shorten the practice and hold only stage two, the middle level. Take a deep breath in, exhale down to stage two, the midway point, and take the longest, deepest five breaths in and out that you can while at that height. Any sensations you feel in the muscles, redirect them to the breath and to the will to remain there.

This is one of the basic trainings of Tai Chi masters, who are said to be unliftable, immovable, totally rooted.

After that fifth and final breath, inhaling, raise up, relax the arms, and shake out the legs.

## Garuḍāsana — The Eagle

> *PRACTICE — garuḍāsana (eagle pose): both sides, lowering with each exhalation*

Our final *āsana* is *Garuḍāsana*, the eagle pose. This can be a very strong, heat-generating pose. And because of its asymmetry, it offers interesting challenges and feedback in our ability to remain balanced when our body is contorted and imbalanced. It is a good reflection of how we are most of the time in our usual life.

Shift the weight onto the balancing foot of your choice, wrapping the other leg from the top and around. The arm on the side of the standing foot comes forward at a right angle in front of the body. The other arm wraps under and around, coming towards *praṇām āsana*, the palm touching the third eye, if possible.

Take very deep breaths here and, optionally, if you wish, lower a few centimetres with each exhalation, holding the position with each inhalation.

With the next breath in, unwind the arms, then the legs. Spend a few moments with both feet on the floor, and feel into your whole body. Balancing practices are wonderful for stirring up ego and emotions. They give us something to look at.

Now, shifting the weight to the opposite leg, repeat the practice. Recall the option of lowering with each breath out, maintaining with each breath in.

With the next breath in, unwind the arms, unwind the legs, stand on both feet, and observe the body. Give the arms a light shake, the legs a jiggle.

## Nāḍī Śodhana with Kapālabhāti

> *PRACTICE — nāḍī śodhana prāṇāyāma with kapālabhāti: one round, 15 breaths on each side, then both*

Come into a comfortable sitting position, suitable for *prāṇāyāma* or meditation. We do just one round of *Nāḍī Śodhana Prāṇāyāma* with *Kapālabhāti*. It will be fifteen *kapālabhāti* breaths on the left side, then the right side, then both nostrils open. If you're not familiar with *Kapālabhāti Prāṇāyāma*, simply breathe in, out, in, out.

And begin. Fifteen rounds on the left.

At the end of those fifteen, take a deep breath in, then out through the right. Fifteen with the right.

At the end of the fifteen with the right, deep breath in, breathe out through the left. And finally fifteen breaths with both nostrils, the hands resting in a *mudrā* on the knees.

### Strong, fit, capable, healthy

When you finish, take a final deep breath in and out, and then a natural breath, with awareness at the eyebrow centre. To how you already feel, add the clearest memory of being strong, fit, capable and healthy. All of the senses purified, and able to perceive the world clearly, perfectly. And a balanced mind and heart.
