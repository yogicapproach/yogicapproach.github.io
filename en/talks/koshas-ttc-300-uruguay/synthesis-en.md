## The Koshas as Lenses, Not Boxes

The series opens by refusing the most common way the koshas are taught. They are not five containers to be memorized and stacked, but a set of instruments for seeing. "I want you to see and experience the koshas as a framework — like a photographer who has a bag full of different lenses and puts on a different lens depending on the situation and the need. We want to be able to direct our awareness fluidly among the various koshas." [1] The skill being trained is not recall but movement: changing the lens on demand.

This reframing is deliberate, and it recurs. Traditional presentations have "immense value, because they are time-tested," yet they can be "overly simplistic," leaving it to the practitioner to align them to lived experience [1]. By the second session the point has sharpened into a correction: "Koshas are not ideas. They are real spaces, real fields." [2] Everything that follows — the metaphors, the measurements, the mantras — is an attempt to make that claim experiential rather than theoretical. The stakes are practical even at the level of a single class. Without this felt dimension, the teacher warns, "settling at the beginning of a class becomes a ritual rather than a practice that generates real change." [1]

## From a Traditional Map to a Living Reframe

Across the first two sessions each sheath receives both its classical description and a modern lens that renders it usable. The physical body, annamaya, the "food sheath," is reframed as a feat of cohesion against dissolution: "The physical body — the Annamaya kosha — is a system of energy conversion." [1] Its solidity is real but not fundamental — "A whirlpool is not different from the water — it is water — but it has a definite form and structure. That is what the human body is: a field of energy that has a definite form and a definite structure." [1]

The energetic sheath, pranamaya, becomes the faculty of shifting between states: "Your ability to shift on demand, to move from one situation to another, is called a state transition." [1] The mental sheath, manomaya, is recast as attention management — "What lets you respond to the changes within that landscape is the capacity to direct attention, which is within the field of the Manomaya kosha." [1] The wisdom sheath, vijnanamaya, is pattern recognition: "Pattern recognition is the root of the whole field of yoga, of Ayurveda, of all the modern and ancient sciences." [1] And the bliss sheath, anandamaya, is baseline coherence — a harmony that, remarkably, is uncovered rather than added.

The second session supplies the axes that make the whole set comparable: scale and density. Each sheath is "orders of magnitude larger than the prior" [2] — the body a meter or two, the pranic field reaching "hundreds of meters into the distance," the mind ranging across time and imagined space, the wisdom body encompassing all that can be known, and the bliss body opening onto the infinite. Density runs the opposite way, from the solid body toward the subtler fields, until the questions themselves become the practice — is there anywhere the mind cannot go; is there anything the space of knowledge does not touch.

## Driving the Car — the Metaphor That Carries the Series

One image runs the length of the training: you, driving. The physical and energetic bodies are the car itself — chassis and transmission. The mind is how you handle the road. Wisdom is the choice of when and where to drive at all. And bliss is the felt sense of every system running in harmony at once. The metaphor is not decorative; it distributes responsibility correctly across the layers. A great car you can drive still will not save you from every situation — when "the leopard jumps onto the road," what responds is directed attention, the work of manomaya. The calmer driver goes further still, checking the weather and choosing not to drive when the leopards are out — the pattern-reading of vijnanamaya.

The image evolves as the series does. Where the first session drives the car, the third listens to it — each Sanskrit letter mapped to a "leaf" of the nervous system, so that a mantra becomes a way of playing the vehicle's own keyboard. The fourth session hands over the keys, then quietly swaps the car for a table: "The first four koshas represent the four legs, and Anandamaya is the balance, the stability of the whole table." [4] The teacher is explicit that the model matters less than the practice of using one: "Find a system that works for you to really connect with the state of each of your koshas — and a model that really works for you, whether it's a car, a table, a bridge, whatever." [4]

## A Metabolic Arc — From the Food Sheath to the Boundless Ground

Read in sequence, the sheaths trace an energetic pattern from the densest, most personal layer toward an underlying ground. The body is "condensed energy"; prana is that energy "increased" by clearing what obstructs it — "we clear the blocks, we increase the flow… We're just conduits for life force — speakers for this music called life." [2] The mind and the wisdom body carry the same current into ever-wider fields, and at the far end the bliss sheath is reached not by addition but by subtraction of interference.

This is the series' most quietly radical claim. Peace is not a destination but a baseline: "We are intrinsically beings of peace — peace is not something we have to add to ourselves; we only have to manage the other distractions." [1] It follows that no single layer can be over-tended at the expense of the rest — "The Anandamaya kosha can truly be experienced when the other four koshas are functioning harmoniously." [1] The same logic answers where emotions live: nowhere and everywhere. "I don't think emotions are in any one kosha, but a phenomenon that arises and moves through them." [1] The value of the framework is precisely that it lets a phenomenon move — and lets a practitioner notice which layer a disturbance is really coming from. The insight generalizes: "Phenomena scale at every layer — what we see in the microcosm is possibly happening in the macrocosm at another scale." [1]

## Wakefulness, Willpower, and Reading the Gunas

If the first session maps the sheaths, the second asks how awake we are in each of them — and whether they can be woken not only one at a time but together. The teacher distinguishes two kinds of alarm: "There are at least two types of alarms within us — the ones that we set, and the ones nature has set for us, out of love. We want to gradually replace all of nature's alarms with our own programming." [2] Wakefulness here means presence, not activity; the horizon question is whether we can "move toward their full potential — not only individually, but simultaneously." [2] This is where willpower enters — not as force but as the patient, deliberate extension of awareness into layers the physical world never requires us to use. Because the body is so easy to exercise, "physical yoga has taken over 99% of the world. But we have four other koshas to be developed to the same capacity." [2]

The practical instrument for this is the reading of the gunas — tamas, rajas, sattva — before any technique is chosen. "When we work with the koshas, always read the gunas. Which guna is predominant in this kosha right now?" [2] The read comes first; the practice is the tuning that follows. This turns the whole toolkit from a list of things to do into something adaptive: "Every single yogic practice is designed to either stimulate, tranquilize, or maintain balance of each of the koshas. They're no longer something to be done, but algorithms to tune ourselves to any moment in time and situation." [2] The same discernment applies to leading others. Ask a room to be still and "three layers immediately emerge: the layer that refuses to be still; the layer that moves into unconsciousness; and the layer that stays attentively present." [2] Reading which layer is in front of you is the beginning of teaching — which is why the connective principle of the second session is so blunt: "Don't teach anything that you haven't touched yourself." [2]

## Sound, Mantra, and the Seeds of Sankalpa

The third session turns from theory to vibration. "One of the main themes of today's class is the power of sound, and how we're going to be experimenting with one of the yogic tools called mantras for working with the koshas." [3] The mechanism offered is physiological as much as devotional: "Each letter of the Sanskrit alphabet maps directly to one of the 'leaves' of these nerve structures throughout the body. So when you do a mantra… you're playing the pranic keyboard of your subtle and gross nervous system." [3] Because sound is bound to prana, and prana pervades every layer, a mantra reaches all five at once — "All life seems to respond to sound and frequency — because it's completely connected to the pranic level, and prana is life." [3]

What gives the vibration direction is sankalpa — a seed of intention planted in the quiet states after settling. "It's in those quieter states… that we would ideally start to plant the seeds of change, through affirmations, through sankalpa." [3] Three mantras are offered, each held with its own intention and mapped to a set of sheaths: the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya at the navel for the grosser koshas, the Gāyatrī at the heart for the mental koshas, and the 32 Names of Durgā for the causal body. At the deepest level the inner dialogue itself changes — "Less 'what change do I want to bring into my life?' and more 'what am I being invited to experience — what has been prepared for me?'" [3] Sound is placed in a long lineage: OM as "the first manifest aspect of unmanifest reality," echoed by "In the beginning was the Word," and the mantras themselves "intuited by rishis — spiritual scientists of the past." [3]

All of this is held within the craft of the observer, the drashta. "One of the pillars of practicing or teaching yoga is the quality of being the observer — the drashta… cultivate an attitude of giving space for someone to have an experience, rather than overly directing it." [3] Working with rising inner energy is treated with the same care one gives a powerful reaction — "This is essentially what we're doing with fusion energy: extremely potent, extremely dangerous if not contained." [3] The counsel throughout is patience and simultaneity: "Strengthen all the koshas gradually and simultaneously… when we take care of the koshas that are easier to work with, the others begin to open up — on their own, when we're ready." [3]

## Integration Through Practice

The final session is deliberately practice-based — "I wanted to make this final session more practice-based" [4] — gathering the framework into tools that can be run in an ordinary day. Sound keeps its central place, now grounded in the elements: "Because sound is one of the subtlest sensory pathways, it's considered to pervade the others." [4] Mantra is named as "a tool of Tantra… By Tantra I mean anything that expands consciousness and liberates energy." [4]

The session's central emphasis is that a sankalpa is not merely words: "I want to emphasize the setup of the mantras: the sankalpa or affirmation, and in particular the waking-up of the emotional feeling of that sankalpa, and the visualization of it." [4] The aim is a trained capacity to feel what one imagines — "we'd like to feel cold when we think cold, start sweating when we think hot" [4] — and it is planted at the moment the mind is most porous. "At the time of waking, we still have contact with our subconscious… as soon as we start to move — or to talk, think, or interact — those dreams slip away faster." [4] The claim is neither small nor merely traditional: the subconscious "could be influencing 70% or more of our waking life," [4] and the ancient teaching finds a modern echo in neuroplasticity — the teacher points readers to Norman Doidge's *The Brain That Changes Itself*, where "visualization can actually change the body." [4]

The practices themselves demonstrate the whole model at once. A set of five asanas, done dry, works several sheaths simultaneously — movement for the body, breath and exertion for the energetic field, coordination and counting for the mind — because "yogic sequences are designed in larger patterns to touch multiple layers at the same time. They're really genius — never just what appears on the surface as physical exercise." [4] The physical and energetic bodies are finally framed as one: "Allow yourself to feel and imagine the physical and energetic body as expressions of each other — not two distinct layers, but a symbiotic system." [4] A Yoga Nidra carries a sankalpa through progressive relaxation; Antarmouna trains the witnessing of the mind; and the Gāyatrī work models the right attitude toward wisdom — "not forcing a solution, but being open to seeing a perspective that may not have been visible." [4] Even the making of intention is named as the practice of the deepest sheath: "Perfecting the art of making sankalpas is itself a practice of the causal body, of Anandamaya kosha… what is it that I really, really want to experience, at its seed?" [4]

## The Koshas in a Modern Life — and an Invitation

The framework is offered, finally, as something to live with rather than to admire. The closing image asks for ongoing maintenance: a table "even with three legs will balance — until you knock out another leg, or put something on the corner that doesn't have a leg under it." [4] The remedy is self-evaluation — of body, energy, mind and wisdom — daily, weekly, monthly, kept honest by a journal. The teacher's own account of collapse, after years spent tending every layer but the emotional one, gives the metaphor its weight; the fall is real when a leg is ignored.

The series consistently meets the modern world on its own terms — entropy and energy conversion, infrared imaging of the pranic field, neuroplasticity, systems thinking, even a wry nod to asking ChatGPT to list the components of a kosha. None of it displaces the tradition; all of it is used to make the tradition legible now. The closing note is an invitation more than a conclusion: to strengthen all the sheaths together and trust the subtler ones to open in time, to teach only what one has touched, and to keep exercising the four koshas that the physical world lets us neglect. "You do it by doing it," [3] the teacher says of intuition — and the same is true of the whole framework. It becomes real only in use.
