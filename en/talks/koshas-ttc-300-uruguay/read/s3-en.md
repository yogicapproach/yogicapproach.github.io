## Framing

Welcome back, for this third out of four sessions on the topic of *koshas*. It has been my intention to include other aspects of yoga philosophy — and even practices — throughout these sessions, with the intention of modeling the interconnectedness of the *koshas* to the entire yoga *vidya*, and the need for us to ground theory in actual practices that we can bring into daily life. Because for many of us, our daily lives are dynamic, in varying environments, the ongoing experiment and *sadhana* is how to bring the essence of a practice into any setting, any time of day or night.

> *PRACTICE — Waking: Kapalabhati + body-shaking → Shavasana*

I'm going to invite you to stand up and clear a little space — enough to be able to come down into *Shavasana* or sit comfortably for at least three to four minutes after the short practice. The practice itself is **Kapalabhati breathing with body-shaking**. Find a rhythm you can sustain for about a minute and a half or two minutes. At any time, if your body or breath says to slow down or stop, please listen to that inner voice. Otherwise continue until asked to stop — and when asked to stop, immediately come into stillness in *Shavasana* or a seated position.

Try to get movement into all of your different bones, while doing rhythmic *Kapalabhati*. Move in asymmetrical ways, like you're a puppet being controlled. Find a rhythm you can maintain — it doesn't have to be high-capacity, but it has to last up to two minutes. Be the observer; feel the changes taking place in the different *koshas* as you move through this practice.

Over the next twenty seconds, begin to slow down. Then come to a stop; lie down in *Shavasana* or sitting. Be completely still, natural breath. Notice what you feel at the sensory level, in the body — the intensity of the sensations, their variety, their movement or stillness. Observe the natural breath with the attitude of a witness: you're watching the body breathe itself, and how that breath is naturally managed internally, how it slows back into a rhythmic breath.

Allow the mind to be aware of, but detached from, various sensory input. Can you feel the floor in contact with the body? Can you hear the sounds around and within you? Can you feel the awareness of these sensory inputs actually moving through the sensory organs into the mind — the vibrations of sound creating vibrations of thought?

Take one step farther back and consider: what power is aware of everything that you're aware of? That part of you which is present when you wake up before you remember your name, before you recall where you are. That part of you that is present in your dreams, even when there is no ego identity.

Bring to mind a favorite memory, image, person, or experience. Try to amplify the emotions it evokes — to really feel it in your body when you think of this inspiring, uplifting, or loving idea. Begin to deepen the breath, to breathe more intentionally, into a full yogic breath. Then bring the palms together and move into a good round of palming. After that, from whichever position you're in, give yourself a long spinal stretch — arms up, toes in the opposite direction — and then gradually find your way to a comfortable position to resume the class.

## The Student–Teacher Relationship and the Koshas

Where we left off two days ago was just starting to talk about the student–teacher relationship and its relationship to the *koshas*. One of the last ideas I had mentioned was: we really want to be grounded in our own practice and our own self-awareness, so we can begin to see that externally — to tune into it in others with minimal projection, minimal guessing, really getting a good sense of what's happening in the landscape in front of us. And even though I'm framing this as a teacher–student interaction, of course this is between any two people in any environment.

What we did just now, in a visual form, is a simple and reliable way for me to give my body some attention, to stimulate my *pranas* in the morning, and to create an experience that is the exact opposite of what I'm moving into — which is stillness. This works very well for most of us and has long been observed as a way to enter effortless quiet. And finally, in that stillness and inner quiet, to guide the awareness through a rotation of the various *koshas*.

It's in those quieter states — where we have access to our subconscious and unconscious — that we would ideally start to plant the seeds of change, through affirmations, through *sankalpa*. We can plant them at the body level, one of the grosser external layers; at the pranic level, which pervades every layer; at the mental layer, which modern and ancient psychology both acknowledge as fertile ground for planting mental and psychic seeds; and at the layer of *Vijnanamaya*, which is now global — in the realm of practices such as **prayer**. Various yogis, saints, and practitioners have written that the power of prayer is partly because it connects to something larger than the self, and there's universal power in that — like charging a battery with a bicycle versus the waves of the ocean.

And then finally, there is the possibility, at least in theory, of aligning our *sankalpa* at the causal level — which by definition is where all things manifest from, at their root. At that point the inner dialogue is less *"what change do I want to bring into my life?"* and more *"what am I being guided to experience in this life? What am I being invited to experience — what has been prepared for me?"*

## The Observer, and Knowing the Practices

One of the pillars of practicing or teaching yoga is the quality of being the observer — the *drashta*. To know the purpose of practices thoroughly, to have an experience of them in as much variety as possible — which essentially means having experienced multiple branches of yoga — and to cultivate an attitude of giving space for someone to have an experience, rather than overly directing that person's experience. And of course, that practice has to start from oneself.

Say I observe a wave of anger rising in me — irritation building to frustration building to anger. I know that *Merudandasana* or *Nadi Shodhana* have had positive effects in the past. But I'm also careful not to shut that feeling down, but to work with it to the capacity that I can stay self-regulated — so that whatever inner experience is arising has the largest, safest container to express itself and move out.

This is essentially what we're doing with **fusion energy**: extremely potent, extremely dangerous if not contained. The evolution of that technology is to start in very small laboratory settings, then evolve to where we can supply it to a city, to the whole world. That's what the yogic tools provide: how do I work with these intensely powerful, world-changing internal energies — learn to observe and read their movements, apply the practices in real time to contain them safely, channel them positively, and share that energy with others so they can evolve along their *own* trajectory, their own timeline, not ours. That's a wonderful and sustainable responsibility to cultivate.

## A Survey of Practices Across the Branches of Yoga

Let's quickly go through what practices we have available across some of the classical branches of yoga. *(Contributions invited from the room.)*

**Hatha Yoga** — many of us have been introduced to a variety of *asanas* and *pranayama*. Among *pranayamas*: **Nadi Shodhana** (some traditions call it *Anuloma Viloma*) — considered one *pranayama* you can do your whole life, any time, day or night, with positive benefits; empirically observed to harmonize the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems. Progressed properly over years, it places some of the most demanding stress on the nervous system and trains the mind to remain calm under it — a skill directly transferable to the real world. Also *Ujjayi* (throat breathing, soft sound) and *Bhramari* — there are over thirty: some to stimulate *prana*, some to tranquilize, some to harmonize. Find a workshop or course to survey them, especially once you're established in *asana*.

And the **shatkarmas**, or cleanses: *Neti* (nasal irrigation); *Kunjal* (therapeutic vomiting — drink a liter or two of salt water and bring it back up; worthwhile for anxiety, grief, emotional stuckness, or a difficult history with food; it also develops remarkable control of autonomic functions). Hatha Yoga is largely designed to help us touch the autonomic systems and develop control over them. There's also a shatkarma where you drink salt water and do *asanas* and it passes through within about thirty minutes to an hour — a gentle, broadly safe way to wash the digestive tract: ***Shankhaprakshalana***. If you've never experienced how much influence food has on body and mind, try a simple version: fast two or three nights — just skip dinner, do your *asana* practice in the morning, and journal how your body feels.

**Karma Yoga** — engaging in volunteer activities, or *seva*; through every act of the day, seeing how the personality or ego responds to interactions in the world.

**Raja Yoga** — *pratyahara* practices (sensory engagement and management); *dharana* (focusing the mind intentionally on an object of choice).

**Bhakti Yoga** — *kirtan* and *japa* (repetition of a mantra); and *Pratipaksha Bhavana*, working with generating opposite emotions willfully.

**Kriya Yoga** — its own set of many practices, designed to awaken deep psychic energies but which immediately influence all the *koshas*.

**Jnana Yoga** — practices such as self-inquiry, or **SWAN**: constantly reviewing what this "person called me" has accumulated in terms of patterns — what are my Strengths, my Weaknesses, my Ambitions, my actual Needs? For the body, the mind, relationships, work — you can apply SWAN to any aspect of life.

And this is just scratching the surface — the major branches hold many subtler, less widely taught practices as well.

### Cultivating the Intuitive Body (*Vijnanamaya*)

Let's look at the name itself: *Vijnanamaya kosha* is the wisdom body, or the intuitive body. How does one cultivate intuition? And a prior question: do any of us have a real sense of what that even means? Some people and cultures say that feeling of knowing is embodied in the gut — the gut feeling. Some say it's the heart's deepest, truest voice. Some experience it in the head, but like the third eye — the center of intuition, the *guru chakra*, the inner guide. In all cases, it's coming from within — from a deeper space that bypasses other layers, bypasses pure thinking, intellectualization, even the ego.

So, on one hand, one practice is **feeling into the depths of ourselves**, letting those innermost feelings come to the conscious mind, and gradually extending the trust to act on them. That has been my practice in developing *Vijnanamaya*. It's also what I've observed in masters I've been blessed to learn under — their intuitive faculty is profound. They've advised me in ways where I couldn't see the connection, and I often disregarded the advice and learned from suffering: "wow, they saw that months or years in advance." What did they see or feel that was blind to me? What perspective are they coming from that lets them see a bigger picture?

You're in a forest, trees everywhere, no way out. Take the perspective of a bird — suddenly the outline of the forest becomes clear, the valleys, the rivers; a clearer map. Take the perspective from space — now entire weather systems, entire continents seem to have a pattern. So another practice is **constantly taking the larger perspective** — stepping out of ourselves, allowing ourselves to be the ego interacting in the world, but simultaneously being the witness of everything taking place. We have terms like systems thinking and holistic design for this zoomed-out state.

On a more grounded level: ask yourself what advice you'd give to yourself at age 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, all the way to your current age — what have you accumulated, in terms of actual wisdom, to guide that copy of yourself growing through the world again? I was telling a friend I wanted to spend more time imagining what my future self would tell me now, so I could live with less ignorance or unneeded suffering. And they tweaked it: "No — be still *now* and listen for that voice. Your future self is talking to you right now; listen to it." As soon as I heard that — even saying it now — I can feel it in my skin: oh yeah, it's already happening.

The *Vijnanamaya kosha* is free of this single temporal point of perspective and is larger in all dimensions — so it has to be *exercised*; we have to move into those dimensions. To answer "how do I do it" — you do it by doing it. It's true in martial arts: when you're stuck in linear movement, the teacher says, "you have two dimensions — just start moving in two dimensions until it feels natural." So with any decision: what would my future self say? My child self? My father? My master? — taking on multiple perspectives, seeing them all as counselors, as fragments of ourselves. And one final practice I keep reminding myself of: **read sattvic content from inspirers** — daily, a random page or line, or three pages through a whole book — nourish *Vijnanamaya* through the words and teachings of wise, positive, deeply experienced humans.

Who is one of the oldest teachers all of us know — who has experienced nearly everything that can be experienced, maybe more? **Mother Nature.** She is old — at least 13.8 billion years, in our universe — and at the same time the youngest being we know, because she is born every moment. She's a profoundly deep teacher, the inspirer of everything we humans have ever claimed as our own: anything we have, she already had — and she does it better.

### Akashic Records and *Vijnanamaya*

*(Question: how does the idea of Akasha interconnect with the four koshas — through the Akashic records?)* The Akashic records, for me, are a good example of the space of *Vijnanamaya kosha* — not only a record of everything that has happened, but a space where anything that can be known can be accessed, like the drawers or shelves of a library. Individuals able to traverse the Akashic records can, in my understanding, move through *Vijnanamaya kosha* in very specific ways — they know which floor, which shelf, which book to open, and how to look it up.

And this is one reason it's always been stressed: **strengthen all the koshas gradually and simultaneously**. Intuiting something about someone or yourself, having a deep memory from the very far past, or seeing something of the future in a dream — it can be unsettling. Sometimes we want access to information that's profound or life-changing, but we don't even know how to take care of our basic diet or sleep. So there's a trust that when we take care of the koshas that are easier to work with, the others begin to open up — *Vijnanamaya*, the Akashic records, become available on their own, when we're ready.

## Sound and Mantra

With the few minutes we have left, I want to say a little about sound, and do three mantras once through. Sound is particularly interesting — I could talk for two hours on sound in relationship to yoga, because it's in so many branches.

From the global perspective, sound comes up in many societies' origin stories. The mantra **OM**, for example, is considered the first manifest aspect of unmanifest reality. In the Old Testament, you have lines like *"In the beginning was the Word."* I follow a lot of research and technology headlines; right now they're using **sound as an alternative to light-based technologies**. Even the petals of the *chakras* are considered the sounds of *prana* entering that junction in the body — and each letter of the Sanskrit alphabet maps directly to one of the "leaves" of these nerve structures throughout the body. So when you do a mantra from any spiritual tradition, you're **playing the pranic keyboard of your subtle and gross nervous system**. That's one reason I think all life seems to respond to sound and frequency — because it's completely connected to the pranic level, and *prana* is life.

> *PRACTICE — Three mantras (each held with a sankalpa; translations are symbolic, not literal)*

I don't expect anyone who hasn't been experimenting with mantras to take them on board at first hearing — but, having tested and developed a relationship with them, I feel it's my heartfelt duty to at least introduce them to anyone willing to listen, because they've had very positive effects on me and many people, and they largely bypass the intellect. We'll hear three mantras today — the **Mahāmṛtyuñjaya Mantra** for the two grosser koshas, the **Gāyatrī Mantra** for the mental (higher and lower mind) koshas, and the **32 Names of Durgā** for the causal body. These mantras were intuited by *rishis* — spiritual scientists of the past — who shared their findings; they resonated with others at their level and survived until now because of their benefit. For each, keep a *sankalpa* or affirmation in mind, to give direction to the energies that arise from the vibration.

- **Mahāmṛtyuñjaya Mantra** — awareness at the navel center, the trigger point for *Manipura chakra*, which regulates *prana* through the whole body and brings vitality and health. Imagine a field of safety and protection around you. *(Chanted once; raw Sanskrit not transcribed.)*
- **Gāyatrī Mantra** — vitalizes and nourishes the *Manomaya* and *Vijnanamaya kosha*. Place the awareness at the heart center and connect to someone who gives you the courage and motivation to live life to the fullest, no matter what we're going through. *(Chanted once.)*
- **The 32 Names of Durgā** (*Durgā Dvātriṃśat Nāmamālā*) — for the causal body. *(Chanted once; concludes with* Om Shanti Shanti Shanti, Hari Om.*)*
